# From owner discovery to a paid proposal

Use this reference for founder or owner meetings, AI diagnosis, pre-sales discovery, and proposals whose main job is to help the owner make a business decision.

## 1. Prepare the facts before the meeting

Prepare only the facts that can change the conversation:

- company: revenue logic, core products, customer groups, scale, and recent business moves;
- industry: cycle, seasonality, channel shifts, and common bottlenecks;
- hiring: priority roles, responsibilities, and requirements, because hiring often reveals the real operating pressure.

Turn the evidence into three testable hypotheses. Phrase each as: “I noticed X; I suspect Y is under pressure; the bottleneck may be Z. Is that accurate?” The purpose is not to prove prior knowledge. It is to begin with concrete business facts and invite correction.

## 2. Set the meeting frame

Do not open with an AI landscape, a large demo, or a full solution. State that the meeting will test whether your judgment can help the owner filter options, expose risks, and decide whether a deeper collaboration is worthwhile.

This frame protects both sides: the owner is not trapped in a sales pitch, and the provider does not give away a complete implementation design before the business target, scope, and required inputs are known.

## 3. Move from the surface problem to the owner's business goal

Ask three questions before proposing scope:

1. Why is AI being discussed now rather than six months ago?
2. If the work succeeds, which operating number should look different in three months?
3. Which red line cannot be crossed: reputation, customer experience, team stability, compliance, cash, or something else?

Reflect the answer back as a business judgment: surface symptom, underlying business move, desired change, and non-negotiable boundary. Ask the owner to correct it. This becomes the proposal's opening thesis and acceptance logic.

## 4. Walk the real workflow

Inspect the process from source to owner, not only the visible pain point:

- What is the condition, ownership, and permitted use of upstream data?
- Which rules are in systems, documents, or only in experienced employees' heads?
- Where do people manually move information across systems?
- Which exceptions occur, who catches them, and who carries responsibility when automation is wrong?

Separate confirmed facts, hypotheses, missing samples, and decisions. Do not promise automation for a step whose inputs or exception rules have not been observed.

## 5. Calculate three ledgers

### Operating ledger

Show how saved effort becomes a visible result: reduced headcount, avoided hiring, higher throughput, faster cash conversion, lower media waste, or additional sales capacity. Time saved alone is not a financial return.

### Organizational ledger

Identify who gains, who must review, who may lose authority or feel exposed, who owns errors, and what new work the system creates. Turn these into role design, approval rights, training, and adoption evidence.

### Risk and capability ledger

State what AI cannot reliably do, what requires human approval, the failure modes, and the rollback or stop conditions. Use boundaries to establish credibility and to price the difficult parts of implementation.

## 6. Convert diagnosis into a low-risk paid path

Split the close into two parts:

- unknowns that require a bounded paid diagnosis or sample review;
- one high-value closed-loop pilot that can prove or disprove the core assumption at low risk.

Secure reciprocal inputs before detailed design: named business owner, sample data, access or export rights, working sessions, diagnosis or phase fee, and the review date. A proposal without these dependencies should not promise an outcome that depends on them.

## 7. Maintain momentum after the meeting

Within 24 hours, send a one-page alignment note containing the owner's business goal, confirmed facts, unresolved unknowns, proposed first loop, dependencies, and next decision. Name the author, recipient, due time, delivery channel, and proof of delivery. It should be easy to forward internally.

Within 48 hours, provide one small artifact for the point the owner cares about most: a rough interaction, sample workflow, annotated output, or two-minute walkthrough. Record the owner, recipient, authorized source data, approval status, due time, delivery channel, and proof of delivery. Label assumptions and synthetic data. If the client has not authorized the data or action, deliver a clearly labeled synthetic version or record the blocked dependency and next decision instead of silently dropping the action. The artifact shows speed and direction; it is not a free production system.

## 8. Translate the discovery into the SOW

Map the meeting evidence into the document:

| Discovery evidence | Proposal location |
|---|---|
| Owner business goal and target number | Executive opening and success standards |
| Red lines and failure ownership | Guardrails, permissions, and acceptance |
| Workflow data, tacit rules, and handoffs | Engineering flow and dependencies |
| Operating ledger | Cost comparison and measurable outcomes |
| Organizational ledger | Governance, roles, and adoption plan |
| Risk ledger | Scope boundaries, fallback, and exclusions |
| Low-risk closed loop | First-phase modules, schedule, and live demonstration |
| Reciprocal inputs | Client responsibilities and start conditions |

The proposal should prove the team's judgment with evidence: what the owner is trying to change, why the chosen loop is a credible first proof, what must be true for it to work, how failure stays controlled, and what decision the evidence enables next.

## Failure patterns

- Answering a surface complaint with a full technical solution.
- Presenting a catalogue of tools before agreeing on the business target.
- Calculating ROI from hours saved without showing how those hours become cash, capacity, or avoided cost.
- Hiding adoption conflict, error ownership, permissions, or required client inputs.
- Sending a full unpaid implementation plan with no reciprocal commitment.
- Promising a company-wide transformation before one bounded loop has produced inspectable evidence.
